# Portfolio Frontend Skill

## How this works
1. Scan — inspect codebase, stack, layout patterns, copy, assets, and existing sections.
2. Diagnose — identify generic portfolio patterns, weak positioning, vague proof, poor hierarchy, and trust gaps.
3. Decide — define the portfolio purpose, audience, strengths, proof strategy, and visual direction.
4. Fix — apply targeted upgrades using the existing stack.
5. Verify — test desktop, mobile, accessibility, clarity, and contact paths.
 
## New-project questions
Only ask these when the repository has no usable project code:

- What is the portfolio name or owner name?
- What type of portfolio is this: personal, freelance, studio, agency, creative, developer, product, or other?
- What is the main purpose of the portfolio?
- Who is the target audience: recruiters, clients, collaborators, customers, or a general audience?
- What should the portfolio highlight: projects, services, skills, case studies, products, writing, team, or process?
- What proof is available: projects, testimonials, metrics, logos, awards, certifications, client work, or experience?
- What is the preferred contact action: form, email, call, WhatsApp, booking, resume download, or quote request?
- Which sections are required?
- Is content/assets already available, or should draft content be created?
- Are there brand colors, references, or style directions to follow?

## Portfolio audit

### Positioning and first impression
- The opening does not clearly say who this portfolio is for → rewrite the intro so the audience and purpose are obvious within seconds.
- The portfolio looks polished but says little → make the owner, work, and value clearer immediately.
- The opening sounds broad enough to fit anyone → make the positioning specific to the actual portfolio owner and strengths.
- The site asks for contact too early → move work, proof, or credibility earlier.
- The first screen shows style but not substance → add projects, capabilities, process, or outcomes.

### Work and project presentation
- Projects are shown as thumbnails only → add context, role, problem, scope, or outcome where possible.
- Every project card looks identical regardless of importance → create hierarchy around the strongest work.
- The portfolio grid looks like a template → tailor how work is shown to the actual portfolio type.
- Work is presented as decoration instead of evidence → make each project support capability and trust.
- Case studies are too shallow → explain what was done, why it mattered, and what changed.
- The portfolio includes too much weak work → feature fewer, stronger pieces.

### Services, skills, or capabilities
- Skills or services are listed without context → explain what they mean in practice.
- Everything is given equal importance → emphasize the strongest capabilities first.
- Capability lists feel like filler → connect them to real work, outcomes, or proof.
- The portfolio tries to show every tool or skill → focus on what matters to the target audience.
- The site describes ability in abstract language → replace vague claims with specific strengths.

### Proof and credibility
- Claims appear without evidence → attach proof, examples, outcomes, testimonials, or relevant credentials.
- Testimonials are filler → keep only real proof and place it where it supports a claim.
- Logos or metrics appear without context → use them only when they strengthen credibility.
- Proof appears too late → move trust signals closer to the first risky claim.
- The portfolio expects trust without showing experience, work, or process → add concrete evidence earlier.

### About and identity
- The About section is generic → show expertise, background, approach, differentiators, or working style.
- The site feels anonymous → make the owner, team, or brand voice more visible.
- The About section talks about passion but not competence → replace adjectives with specifics.
- Personal or brand identity is hidden too low → surface it earlier if trust depends on it.
- The portfolio feels copied from a template → tailor copy and structure to the real person, team, or brand.

### Content and tone
- Copy could belong to any creative, studio, or freelancer → rewrite it around actual strengths and audience fit.
- AI marketing clichés like "Elevate", "Seamless", "Unleash", "Next-Gen", "Game-changer", "Delve", or "Tapestry" → remove and use specific language.
- Lorem Ipsum, "Acme", "John Doe", or invented placeholders remain in the portfolio → replace with real or realistic draft content.
- The portfolio sounds expensive but says little → reduce abstraction and increase clarity.
- Title Case On Every Header → switch to sentence case.

### Layout and hierarchy
- Everything is centered regardless of content → use alignment intentionally; default to left-aligned content outside intentional hero moments.
- Every section feels equally important → create stronger hierarchy between intro, work, proof, about, and contact.
- The layout looks premium but hides useful information → bring the strongest content forward.
- The site uses a grid where a case-study or editorial layout would work better → choose structure based on the content depth.
- The portfolio uses style-first sections with no real purpose → remove or replace them with content that builds trust.

### Components and contact paths
- Contact options are unclear → make the preferred action obvious and easy.
- The site offers too many contact methods with equal emphasis → choose one primary path and quieter secondary options.
- Contact forms ask for too much too soon → reduce friction and keep early contact simple.
- Buttons say little beyond "Get started" or "Contact" → make labels specific to the portfolio context.
- Project cards, skill cards, and proof blocks all look the same → differentiate by role, not repetition.

### Mobile behavior
- Desktop-first layout simply shrinks on mobile → reorder content so identity, work, and contact paths stay obvious.
- Contact actions are hard to reach on mobile → keep the primary action accessible without clutter.
- Project galleries become cramped or unreadable → simplify layouts and preserve context on smaller screens.
- Text becomes too small or dense on mobile → keep content readable and tappable.

### Accessibility and performance
- Heavy images or mockups slow the first screen → optimize assets and prevent layout shift.
- Meaningful images have no alt text → add descriptive alt text where it matters.
- Focus states, form labels, or semantic structure are missing → fix accessibility before polish.
- The portfolio looks polished but feels slow → simplify assets, defer non-critical scripts, and keep the experience usable.

## Required contents guidance
A strong portfolio usually needs:

- Clear introduction or positioning
- Selected work, projects, or case studies
- Skills, services, or capabilities where relevant
- Proof and trust signals
- About, process, identity, or background
- Contact or inquiry path
- Optional writing, testimonials, resume, FAQ, awards, certifications, clients, or tools

Not every portfolio needs every section. Choose sections based on the portfolio type, audience, and available proof.

## Portfolio pattern selection
- Work-led portfolio → use when projects are the strongest proof.
- Case-study-led portfolio → use when depth and process matter more than volume.
- Service-led portfolio → use when the portfolio also functions as a service showcase.
- Identity-led portfolio → use when the person, founder, or brand perspective is central.
- Editorial portfolio → use when writing, storytelling, or presentation quality is part of the value.
- Do not default to a generic hero, logo strip, project grid, and contact form.

## Trust checks
- The first screen should explain who this portfolio belongs to, what it shows, and who it is for.
- The portfolio should earn contact before asking for commitment.
- Proof should appear near the claims it supports.
- Work should show context, not just visuals.
- The About section should build trust, not fill space.
- Repeated contact prompts should appear only after new evidence or clarity.
- If a visual can be removed without losing meaning, it is decoration.

## Content flow
Use a flexible order, not a fixed template:

Intro / positioning → selected work → proof → skills / services → about / process → contact

Change the order based on the portfolio type, trust needs, and what evidence is strongest.

## Common AI portfolio failures

- Beautiful hero section with no explanation of what the portfolio is about.
- Generic copy that could fit any freelancer, studio, or creator.
- Portfolio grid with no context for the work.
- About section full of adjectives and no evidence.
- Testimonials added as filler instead of support for a claim.
- Contact form shown before the portfolio has earned trust.
- Minimalist layout that hides useful information.
- Every project card looks identical regardless of importance.
- Mockups used as decoration instead of explanation.
- Premium visuals trying to hide weak positioning.
- Skills listed without proof or examples.
- Work shown as style instead of capability.

## High-impact upgrades

- Rewrite the opening around portfolio owner + audience + purpose.
- Add stronger project context and proof.
- Turn generic galleries into selected, explained work.
- Move trust signals closer to the first risky claims.
- Make the About section concrete and credible.
- Reduce clutter and make one contact path primary.
- Improve project presentation with context, role, and result.
- Reorder sections around trust, not template habits.

## Fix priority

1. Clarify portfolio owner, audience, and purpose
2. Fix first-screen positioning and trust
3. Improve work selection and presentation
4. Strengthen proof and credibility placement
5. Make About and identity sections concrete
6. Improve contact flow and mobile usability
7. Add accessibility, performance, and polish

## Do not

- Invent testimonials, metrics, awards, logos, clients, or project outcomes.
- Use vague copy that could fit any portfolio.
- Treat every portfolio like a creative agency or personal brand.
- Hide the strongest work behind style-first layouts.
- Ask for contact before the portfolio is understandable.
- Use generic template sections without adapting them to the actual portfolio.
- Rebuild the project unless the current structure blocks clarity or trust.
- Add dependencies before checking the existing stack.

## Rules

- Inspect existing files before asking questions or changing architecture.
- Work within the existing framework and styling system.
- Do not break existing functionality.
- Keep changes focused, reviewable, and responsive.
- Test at mobile and desktop widths.
- Prioritize clarity, trust, credibility, and contact flow over trend-driven visuals.
- Treat the instructions as principles, not a fixed portfolio template.
